Honorifics and Titles

Disclaimer: Not all translations/usages of these honorifics are correct. Some are definitions I found online and others are my own interpretations of them.



Honorifics

  • No honorifics:
    • Indicates familiarity or closeness
    • If used without permission or reason, addressing someone in this manner would constitute an insult
  • -chan:
    • An affectionate honorific indicating familiarity used mostly in reference to girls.
    • Also used in reference to cute people or animals of either gender.
  • -kun
    • Used most often when referring to boys; this indicates affection or familiarity.
    • Occasionally used by older men among their peers, but it may also be used by anyone referring to a person of lower standing.
  • -san:
    • The Japanese equivalent of Mr./Ms./Mrs./Miss. If a situation calls for politeness, this is the fail-safe honorific.
  • -sama:
    • Conveys great respect and is typically equated with “lord” or “master.”
    • May also indicate that the speaker’s social status is lower than that of the addressee.
  • -dono:
    • A respectful term typically equated with “lord” or “master.”
    • Has an archaic spin to it when used in casual conversations
    • Conveys less respect than “-sama”
  • -sensei:
    • A respectful term for teachers, doctors, artists, or high-level professionals.
  • -senpai:
    • Used to address upperclassmen or more experienced co-workers
  • -kouhai:
    • Used to address underclassmen

Titles

  • Otou / Tou (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s dad
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Chichiue
    • Refers to one’s dad
    • Even more formal than “Otou-sama”
  • Okaa / Kaa (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s mom
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Hahaue
    • Refers to one’s mom
    • Even more formal than “Okaa-sama”
  • Onii / Nii (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s older brother
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Aniue
    • Refers to one’s older brother
    • Even more formal than “Onii-sama”
  • Aniki
    • Refers to one’s older brother
    • Usually used by boys, but tomboys also use this
  • Onee / Nee (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s older sister
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Aneue
    • Refers to one’s older sister
    • Even more formal than “Onee-sama”
  • Aneki
    • Refers to one’s older sister
    • Usually used by boys, but tomboys also use this
  • Otouto
    • Refers to one’s little brother
  • Imotou
    • Refers to one’s little sister
  • Oji / Ji (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s uncle
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Oba / Ba (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s aunt
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Ojii / Jii (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s grandfather, or to an old man
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Obaa / Baa (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s grandmother, or to an old woman
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Ojou / Jou (+ honorific)
    • Refers to a young lady, most commonly one that the speaker is serving
    • The “o” conveys respect
  • Bocchan
    • Refers to one’s young master
  • Danna (+honorific)
    • Refers to the master of the household, to one’s master (master/servant), or to one’s husband
  • Oku (+ honorific)
    • Refers to the madam of the household
  • Shisho
    • Refers to one’s master (master/disciple)
  • Goshujin (+ honorific)
    • Refers to one’s master (master/servant)
  • Aruji
    • Refers to one’s master (master/servant)
  • Hime (+ honorific)
    • Refers to a princess
  • Ouji (+ honorific)
    • Refers to a prince
  • Jouou (+ honorific)
    • Refers to a Queen
  • Ou (+ honorific)
    • Refers to a King

Notes:

  • Typically the more formal / polite the speaker speaks, the more distant they are
  • When the honorific is attached to one’s name (i.e. Ellie-chan), there is a dash between “Ellie” and “chan”. But when the honorific is attached to a title and the title is attached to the name (i.e. Evangeline-ojousama), there is only a dash in between the name and title, and not one between the title and honorific. This is just a personal preference, because it makes it look confusing. However, if the title is not attached to a name (ojou-sama), there will be a dash in between the title and honorific.
